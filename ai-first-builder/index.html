From UX Designer to AI First Builder

I’ll share how I’ve been expanding my work from traditional UX design into software engineering.

As agentic coding emerged, I began experimenting with becoming a more effective, faster designer - one who can now contribute directly to the codebase, because that is where tangible value is created.

The key milestones:

  • In February 2026, I built and released a macOS app, for viewing Commodore 64 graphics through lifelike CRT emulation. I learned the entire Git workflow and have done everything that way since.
  • In March and April 2026, I redesigned and rebuilt my website with 3D interactions and models, using a CGA shader.
  • In May 2026, I built a personal infrastructure dashboard, complete with an RSS reader and a fully custom architecture, to keep track of my development projects.
  • In June and July 2026, I single-handedly developed an old idea of mine: a social app called You On My Mind, including both the frontend and backend.

You On My Mind

Ten years ago, a friend and I tried to build a social media app together. He originated the idea, while I was responsible for design and product. We hired contractors, had it built and released it - bugs and all - in the Apple and Google app stores. It was a total failure with zero users, but I learned a great deal.

The idea is simple: with this app, you can send someone one message: “I’m thinking of you.” There are no emojis, heartbeats, add-on messages, or conversation, just a simple counter showing how many seconds the other person has been thinking of you. A thought lasts for as long as the sender keeps their finger on the screen.

The whole thing resembles a phone call without the phone call: we see that someone is calling us, get a notification, the seconds tick by, but we cannot speak into it - only watch in silence. There is one thing we can do, though: press back into the call, so we can do it together.

It is a wonderfully funny little thing, and there is a reason we have not let the idea go cold all these years.

This was what I began pitching: I was looking for a developer who would help me build the app. I did not find anyone, despite speaking to lots of people. Everyone liked the idea, but no one wanted to commit to it.

Then I thought I would build the frontend for the interaction above myself, then start looking for a backend developer. I got it done in about a week, mocking the main interactions: what happens when I have only a few friends, when I have more, when someone is thinking of me, when several people are thinking of me at once, when someone is thinking of me just as I am thinking of someone else, and so on.

The video above shows the state once the main interactions were in place: incoming and outgoing calls, plus the touching-fingers event (where we “press back” into an incoming call).

You can select the main use cases from a simple admin menu. I decided from the start that I would design and test the iPhone app on a physical device in every case. Fortunately, Apple’s tooling makes this remarkably convenient, and I could try each build on the device immediately.

The backend was still a problem, though, because an app with this level of complexity raises a few questions:

  • How is user identity created? Do people need to register with a username and password, like it is the Stone Age?
  • Where will the central server live? What will the deployment process be? What will the database be?
  • How can several users be served simultaneously, with the experience synchronized to the second?

I decided to take it on alone and start very lean: buy an inexpensive VPS, then deploy a server from Docker which, at first, only returns “I am alive” through a simple API. After that, I would continue step by step, in a sequence of verifiable lean increments.

A programmer friend suggested I simplify it and try Supabase. It has a Realtime layer, which is exactly right for this because I would not have to fiddle with the socket work by hand. Its free plan is pretty good too.

So I set out using Supabase. The first problem I needed to solve was being able to sign in and out of the app. Nothing else. Apple became the identity provider, and then I created the user in my user table. From that point, I had an active session.

The first thing I implemented was allowing users to rename themselves, persist the change, and load it back from the table:

And naturally, there had to be Row Level Security, in keeping with modern practice. The next problem was uploading and changing my own avatar on the server.

Then came the Realtime layer itself, establishing a direct connection between two users. I took many small steps and made each one verifiable before moving to the next phase. It was the only way to keep the system consistently testable, coherent, and working:

From there, many more steps followed before I could actually “yomm” (as I call this new thing) with other people - and receive their yomms as well.

For the design work, I chose an ultra-lean approach. I focused only on the problem immediately in front of me. When the shoe started to pinch, I moved on. The settings screen began with two buttons, then I added a few more, and eventually it looked like this when I decided to redesign it:

To redesign it, I simply showed the screen to an agent and asked for a few variations.

From there, I continued iterating in code, tuning the small details and emphasis directly.

I did not abandon Figma either; the larger UI refactors are better done there. For example, I am planning a feature that lets you yomm someone secretly. I imagine it will be used more by high-school students: say a boy wants to send a girl a small “I’m thinking of you” gesture without revealing who he is. By the way, the app came together end to end this summer in roughly two months of work. The plumbing is in place; everything works. I also ran a TestFlight among people I know, where they can install it themselves, but it is fairly cumbersome for people unfamiliar with TestFlight and its many steps.

My plan now is to build the website after implementing the Secret Yomm mode above, then simply release the app on the App Store. By my calculations, Supabase’s free plan can serve 30 to 40 concurrent yomms. If I reach that point - meaning I have to start paying for Supabase - I will consider the experiment a success.

Developing and designing an app of this complexity alone is a wonderful way to spend time, and I hope that anyone who tries it will enjoy using it.
